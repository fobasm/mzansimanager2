Squad Rotation, Morale and Injuries

Many managers lose seasons not because their squad is weak, but because they run it badly. Tired players, injuries and unhappy stars quietly cost points. This guide shows how to keep your squad fit, fresh and motivated through a long season.

Why rotation matters

A league season has 38 matches, and the cup competitions add more. Players who play every game become tired, and tired players perform below their rating and are more likely to be injured. A well-run club rotates its squad so that the best players are fresh for the biggest games, while the others keep their match sharpness.

Check stamina before every game

Before each match, look at the stamina of your starting eleven on the squad or lineup page. If a key player is low on stamina, consider resting him, especially if the next opponent is weak or if a more important match is coming up. The best time to rotate is before a cup game against a lower-ranked side, or when you have already built a lead in the league.

Injuries

Injuries are a normal part of the game. When a player is injured, he is unavailable for a number of days, and the squad page shows how long he will be out. Do not rush injured players back. Instead, make sure that you have a capable replacement for every position. This is why the transfer guides recommend two good options for each place in the team. If a long-term injury hits a key position, consider a free agent signing in the next window.

Cards and suspensions

Yellow and red cards lead to bans. A red card means a suspension, and every fifth yellow card a player collects in a season also brings a one-match ban. Before each match, check whether any of your players is one booking away from a ban. If so, think about whether to start him, use him as a substitute or rest him. High pressing increases the number of cards, so a team that presses a lot needs a deeper squad.

Playing time and morale

Every player has a mood. Players who are in the team and who are winning tend to be happy. Players who think they should be playing but sit on the bench become unhappy, and the game takes into account how many unhappy players there are when it assesses the dressing room. A squad with several unhappy players performs worse and may lose key people when their contracts end.

The ranking of a player in your squad affects how much playing time he expects. A top-eleven player expects to start most games, whereas a player outside the top sixteen expects very little. Manage the expectations by giving fringe players regular cup games and by promoting young players only when they have a realistic route into the team.

Results and morale

Winning raises morale, and losing streaks lower it. If you hit a bad run, resist panic changes. Check the facts first: are you losing because of tired players, because of an injury to a key position or because of a poor tactical match-up? Fix the actual cause, and the morale will recover with the results.

Using the squad depth

A squad of between 22 and 26 players is enough for most managers. Keep at least two goalkeepers. Make sure that every outfield position has cover, ideally a player who can play in more than one place. Players who can cover two positions are very valuable when injuries strike.

A weekly routine

  1. Check injuries and suspensions on the squad page.
  2. Check stamina for the starting eleven.
  3. Decide who to rest, thinking about the next two fixtures, not only the next one.
  4. Review tactics against the opponent.
  5. After the game, look at player ratings and form to spot players who are struggling.

Contracts and morale

A player with a short contract may be easier to lose. Renew the contracts of your important players well before they expire, and make sure that the wage you offer fits the club's finances. If you cannot afford to keep a player, sell him before his deal expires so that you get a fee instead of nothing. The transfer market guide explains how.

Common mistakes

  • Playing the same eleven in every game.
  • Ignoring a player who is one yellow card from a ban.
  • Keeping a thin squad with no backup goalkeeper.
  • Letting several unhappy players build up on the bench.
  • Making a lot of changes in the middle of a bad run instead of finding the cause.
